# Resignation Strategy

> Leave so well that your former team would rehire you — and your reputation travels intact.

---

## Purpose

Guide senior engineers through resignation planning, notice periods, knowledge transfer, counteroffers, exit interviews, and relationship maintenance — especially in markets where ex-colleagues become future hiring managers.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Resign only after written offer (and any contingencies you accept).
- Counteroffers rarely fix the root cause; most who accept leave again within a year.
- Knowledge transfer is your last professional delivery — treat it like a project.
- Exit interviews: honest but non-scorching.
- LinkedIn updates wait until you’ve started (usually).

## Planning Resignation

### Sequence

1. Written offer signed / accepted in writing.
2. Background contingencies understood.
3. Personal runway and start date aligned with notice.
4. Resignation conversation with manager (verbal) + formal letter.
5. KT plan agreed within 48 hours.
6. Wind down access and handoff.

### Decision framework — when to tell whom

| Who | When |
|-----|------|
| Manager | First, live if possible |
| Skip-level | If manager unavailable; else after manager |
| Team | After manager aligns on message |
| Wider peers | As needed for handoff |
| Social media | After start (default) |

## Notice Period

Honor contractual notice unless both parties agree otherwise. Senior roles often have 30 days; some enterprises 60+. Buying out notice is a company-to-company discussion — don’t ghost.

### Professional communication — resignation meeting

> I’ve accepted a Lead role elsewhere and am resigning. My last day per notice is [date]. I’m committed to a clean handoff of [systems]. I’ll send a formal letter after this conversation.

### Resignation letter template

```text
[Date]

Dear [Manager],

Please accept this letter as formal notice of my resignation from the position of [Title] at [Company]. 
Per my notice period, my last working day will be [Date].

I appreciate the opportunities to work on [1–2 specifics] and will support an orderly transition.

Sincerely,
[You]
```

## Knowledge Transfer

### KT project plan (senior-owned)

| Workstream | Artifact |
|------------|----------|
| Systems owned | Architecture sketch + runtime dependencies |
| On-call / incidents | Top incidents, dashboards, runbooks |
| In-flight work | Status, risks, next decisions |
| Access & secrets | Transfer via proper vault/IT — never chat apps |
| People map | Who to ask for what |
| Unwritten rules | Deploy windows, approvers, compliance quirks |

### Checklist

- [ ] Successor or interim owner named
- [ ] Runbooks updated where you were the bus factor
- [ ] PRs merged or explicitly handed
- [ ] Calendar: cancel recurring meetings you owned or reassign
- [ ] Docs linked from a single index page

### Real-world example

Lead on a telco billing integration leaves behind a one-page “month-end war room” guide: job sequence, failure modes, escalation tree. The team survives the next cycle without pages to the departed engineer — that is professionalism.

## Counteroffers

### Why companies counter

Retention optics, project risk, cost of replacement — not sudden enlightenment about your value.

### Decision framework

Stay only if **the root cause changes structurally** (manager change, scope change, written comp + level, timeline). A raise alone rarely repairs broken trust or dead learning slopes.

### Script — declining counteroffer

> I appreciate the counteroffer. My decision is based on [scope / domain / career thesis], not only compensation. I’m committed to leaving on [date] and making the transition smooth.

### Common mistakes

- Using an offer only to fish a counter (burns trust if discovered).
- Accepting a counter, then facing resentment and stalled promotion.
- Revealing the new employer’s confidential package details unnecessarily.

## Maintaining Relationships

- Thank mentors specifically.
- Offer limited post-exit help windows only if appropriate (and cleared with new employer policy).
- Connect on LinkedIn with personalized notes.
- Never trash the company in public or private Slack screenshots.

---

## Exit Interviews

### Good content

- Process improvements, resourcing truths, what would retain seniors next time.
- Specific and constructive.

### Avoid

- Personal attacks.
- Confidential new-employer information.
- Burning individuals who can’t respond.

### Example

> Seniors leave when architecture decisions lack an owner and on-call is unpaid labor. A written on-call stipend and clearer platform ownership would have changed retention conversations.

## Professionalism

| Do | Don’t |
|----|-------|
| Keep delivering until last day | Coast / delete work |
| Stay calm if emotions rise | Boast about the new TC |
| Return hardware promptly | Take proprietary code |
| Clarify garden leave / non-compete | Violate non-solicit casually |

---

## LinkedIn Updates

Default: update job after you’ve started and informed close colleagues. Keep tone grateful, not smug.

> Excited to join [Company] as [Title], working on [domain]. Grateful to [Previous Company] teammates for [specific growth].

---

## Starting the Next Role

- Rest 2–5 days if feasible between jobs (cognitive reset).
- Re-read offer constraints (IP, moonlighting, non-compete).
- Prep a personal 30-60-90 outline before day one (next chapters).
- Don’t recruit ex-colleagues immediately if non-solicit applies.

---

## Common Mistakes

- Resigning on verbal offer only.
- Shortening notice unilaterally.
- Leaving bus-factor landmines.
- Rage-quitting after a counteroffer meeting.
- Posting memes about “escape” while still employed.
