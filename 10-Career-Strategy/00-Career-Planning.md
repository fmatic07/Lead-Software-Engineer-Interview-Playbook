# Career Planning

> Choose roles that compound career capital — not just titles that look good on LinkedIn.

---

## Purpose

Give senior engineers a decision system for *where* to go next: company type, manager quality, work mode, and the learning-vs-compensation tradeoff. At Lead / Tech Lead / Architect level, a wrong move costs 18–36 months of trajectory — not just a paycheck.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Career vision is a 3–5 year capability thesis, not a job title wishlist.
- Manager quality and problem difficulty outweigh brand prestige for most mid-career moves.
- Enterprise, startup, and govtech optimize different forms of career capital.
- Remote/hybrid/onsite is a leverage decision, not a lifestyle preference alone.
- Personal brand and continuous learning are risk hedges when markets tighten.

## Career Vision

### Explanation

Write a one-paragraph thesis: *In 3–5 years, I want to be the person who can X, trusted for Y, in environments like Z.* Examples of X: “own payment domain architecture end-to-end,” “lead platform migrations with zero-downtime cutovers,” “set API and data standards across three product teams.”

Title chasing (“I want to be Staff”) without a capability thesis produces lateral moves that feel like promotions.

### Decision framework

| Dimension | Question | Signal of clarity |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| Capability | What hard problems will I be known for? | Can name 2–3 problem classes |
| Environment | Regulated enterprise, product scale-up, or govtech? | Can reject roles that don’t fit |
| Scope | Team lead, domain architect, or org-wide staff? | Matches how you actually work |
| Constraints | Location, visa, family, risk tolerance | Explicit walk-aways |

### Real-world examples

- **Fintech (Maya/GCash-class):** Vision = “own ledger correctness and reconciliation under regulatory scrutiny.”
- **Telco (Globe-class):** Vision = “design high-availability billing integrations across legacy and cloud.”
- **Product (Canva/Atlassian-class):** Vision = “raise engineering bar on a high-scale multi-tenant product.”
- **GovTech SG:** Vision = “ship citizen-facing systems with security, auditability, and public accountability.”

### Common mistakes

- Confusing “bigger title at a weaker company” with progress.
- Optimizing only for current stack comfort (Spring forever) instead of problem difficulty.
- No written walk-away criteria before recruiter conversations start.

## Choosing the Right Company

### Explanation

Evaluate companies on *problem quality, engineering maturity, and organizational honesty* — not glassdoor star averages alone.

### Decision framework — Company Fit Scorecard

Score each 1–5. Weight by your thesis (default weights shown).

| Criterion | Weight | What “5” looks like |
|-----------|--------|---------------------|
| Problem difficulty & blast radius | 20% | Real production scale, money/regulatory risk |
| Engineering culture | 15% | Code review, on-call, ADRs, blameless postmortems |
| Manager / org quality | 20% | Clear priorities, protects focus, develops people |
| Architecture maturity | 10% | Intentional debt, not accidental complexity |
| Growth / promotion path | 10% | Documented levels, recent promotions of ICs |
| Comp competitiveness | 10% | At or above market for your level |
| Stability / runway | 10% | Clear funding, profitable unit, or public mandate |
| Mission / domain interest | 5% | You can care for 3+ years |

**Rule:** Reject if Manager or Problem Difficulty scores ≤2, regardless of total.

### Real-world examples

- Strong brand, weak manager → you become a ticket-taker on a legacy monolith.
- Unsexy domain (claims, KYC, settlement) with excellent leads → career capital compounds fast.
- Hypergrowth startup with no on-call discipline → learning is chaotic, not transferable.

## Choosing the Right Manager

### Explanation

At senior levels, your manager controls scope, visibility, and whether your work is attributed correctly. Interview *them* as hard as they interview you.

### Decision framework — Manager Diligence

Ask in hiring-manager loops:

1. “What does success look like in the first 90 days for this role?”
2. “What decisions will I own vs need alignment for?”
3. “How do you handle technical disagreements on the team?”
4. “Who was promoted recently, and what did they demonstrate?”
5. “What is currently on fire, and why?”

### Signals

| Green | Red |
|-------|-----|
| Specific 90-day outcomes | Vague “hit the ground running” |
| Names real constraints (debt, headcount, compliance) | Only talks culture slogans |
| Discusses how they unblocked someone | Takes credit for team wins |
| Clear on-call / incident expectations | “We don’t really have incidents” |

### Professional communication example

> “I’m trying to understand ownership boundaries. For the payments migration you mentioned — would I own the cutover plan and rollback criteria, or is that held by the platform architect?”

## Enterprise vs Startup

### Decision framework

| Factor | Enterprise / Bank / Telco | Startup / Scale-up | GovTech / Public |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------|------------------|
| Career capital | Process, compliance, scale, stakeholder management | Breadth, speed, ambiguity tolerance | Security, audit, citizen impact |
| Risk | Slower promotion, politics | Runway / pivot / layoff | Procurement, policy shifts |
| Comp shape | Base + bonus + benefits | Base + equity lottery | Base + stability + mission |
| Best for | Deep domain + leadership under constraints | Generalist ownership, 0→1 | Mission + rigorous engineering |

### Common mistakes

- Joining a Series A for “equity upside” without reading dilution, preference stack, or refresh policy.
- Leaving enterprise for a startup solely because of boredom — without checking engineering bar.
- Treating govtech as “slow enterprise” when the real differentiator is accountability and security posture.

## Remote vs Hybrid vs Onsite

### Explanation

Work mode affects influence loops: architecture debates, incident command, and political capital. Fully remote seniors must be deliberate about visibility.

### Decision framework

| Mode | Choose when | Cost |
|------|-------------|------|
| Remote | Strong async culture, written ADRs, clear owners | Harder informal influence; timezone tax |
| Hybrid | Need whiteboard depth + some home focus | Commute + meeting gravity toward office days |
| Onsite | Early team formation, heavy pairing, regulated desk work | Geography lock; slower personal life flexibility |

### Real-world example

Distributed Grab/Atlassian-style teams reward engineers who write crisp RFCs. Onsite fintech war rooms reward engineers who can facilitate live incident decisions. Match your influence style to the mode.

## Compensation vs Learning

### Decision framework — 24-Month Value

Estimate:

`24-month value ≈ cash TC + skills premium + network premium − burnout cost − opportunity cost`

- **Cash TC:** base + bonus + equity (haircut startup equity aggressively).
- **Skills premium:** will this role make you hireable at +15–25% later?
- **Network premium:** managers/peers who open doors.
- **Burnout cost:** if the role destroys capacity, learning is fake.

### Rule of thumb

Accept a temporary cash discount only if the role clearly upgrades *problem difficulty* or *scope of ownership*. Never discount cash for “cool brand” with shallow work.

## Career Capital

### Explanation

Career capital = scarce, valuable skills + credible proof + trusted relationships.

For Java enterprise seniors, high-capital skills include: production incident command, data consistency under failure, migration leadership, security-conscious design, and cross-team API governance.

### Checklist — Capital Audit

- [ ] Can name 3 production decisions with metrics
- [ ] Have mentees who would vouch for your leadership
- [ ] Have written artifacts (ADRs, runbooks, RFCs) you can discuss
- [ ] Have one domain where you are “the person to call”
- [ ] Have relationships outside your current team

## Personal Branding

### Explanation

Brand is *consistent public proof of judgment* — not motivational posts. Useful surfaces: technical writing, conference/internal talks, open source, well-structured LinkedIn experience bullets tied to outcomes.

### Professional communication example (LinkedIn summary)

> Lead Software Engineer focused on payment and ledger systems. I care about idempotency, reconciliation, and boring reliability. Recently led a zero-downtime cutover for settlement batch processing serving X TPS with Y% error reduction.

### Common mistakes

- Inflating titles (“architect” of a single CRUD service).
- Posting hot takes without shipping evidence.
- Invisible brand: strong engineer, zero discoverability for recruiters.

## Continuous Learning

### Practical roadmap (senior+)

| Quarter focus | Example |
|---------------|---------|
| Deepen | Distributed transactions / outbox / saga failure modes |
| Broaden | Observability or security threat modeling |
| Lead | Mentoring loop + architecture review facilitation |
| Signal | One write-up or internal talk |

AI-assisted engineering is now baseline: use it to accelerate boilerplate and exploration; keep judgment, threat models, and production responsibility human-owned.

## Career Risk Management

### Risks and hedges

| Risk | Hedge |
|------|-------|
| Layoff / reorg | 6-month runway savings; warm network; updated narrative |
| Skill obsolescence | Annual learning plan; ship something outside comfort zone |
| Manager dependency | Relationships with skip-level and peer leads |
| Single-company identity | External writing, OSS, community |
| Visa / geography lock | Prefer transferable domain skills over proprietary tooling only |

### Decision framework — Stay vs Leave

Leave when 2+ are true for 2+ quarters:

1. Learning slope is flat.
2. Manager is a blocker to scope/visibility.
3. Comp is >15% below market *and* no path to correction.
4. Values mismatch on ethics, quality, or on-call load.
5. Your health is degrading.

## Reflection Questions

1. If your current company disappeared tomorrow, what scarce skill still makes you hireable?
2. Are you optimizing for comfort or for the next hard problem you want to own?
3. Who is the best manager you have had — and what behaviors will you screen for now?
4. Which career capital are you under-investing in: skills, proof, or relationships?
